The bell rang at 10:17.

For everyone else, it meant the beginning of break. For Andrey, it meant the beginning of the war.

The corridor outside the sixth-form common room erupted instantly. Doors slammed. Shoes squeaked against the polished floor. Someone shouted that they had lost their calculator. Someone else shouted that they had found it. A Year 10 sprinted around the corner at a speed that suggested either extreme urgency or the discovery of a fire.

Andrey stood against the wall, watching it all. He had a notebook tucked underneath one arm, but it hadn't been opened all morning.

Oliver noticed.

“You haven't revised.”

Andrey looked at him. “No.”

“You've got the test tomorrow.”

“I know.”

“You haven't revised for it.”

“I know.”

Oliver stared at him with the quiet horror of someone watching another person deliberately walk into traffic.

“And you're not worried?”

“About what?”

“The test.”

“No.”

Oliver adjusted his glasses. It was irritating. Not because Andrey was lazy. Oliver could tolerate laziness. Not because Andrey was arrogant. Oliver had met arrogant people before.

It was the fact that Andrey could be right.

That was the problem.

Oliver had spent years treating school like a mountain. He climbed every evening. He memorised every specification point, every obscure exception, every mark-scheme phrase that might squeeze an extra two marks from an examiner.

Andrey looked at the mountain and wondered why everyone was climbing it.

“School isn't pointless,” Oliver said.

“I didn't say school was pointless.”

“You said the system was imaginary.”

“It is.”

“That's literally not an argument.”

“It wasn't meant to be.”

Oliver opened his mouth.

Then closed it.

Andrey walked away.

Across the corridor, Ben Negus had begun explaining why Andrey was wrong. Nobody had asked.

That had never stopped him.

“According to authority,” Ben said, raising one finger, “the education system exists for a reason. Therefore, saying it's imaginary is objectively—”

“Appeal to authority,” Andrey called without turning around.

Ben froze.

“What?”

“You said an authority believes something, therefore it's true.”

“I didn't.”

“You did.”

“I was explaining—”

“Appeal to authority.”

Ben frowned. “That doesn't make me wrong.”

“No,” Andrey said. “Your argument makes you wrong.”

Sam, passing behind them, whispered to Dersim, “He's cooked.”

Dersim nodded solemnly. “I have no idea what's happening.”

They continued walking.

Three seconds later, a loud crash came from around the corner.

Andrey sighed.

“That's probably them.”

It was.

A bin lay on its side. Dersim stood beside it. Sam stood beside Dersim. Neither of them appeared particularly concerned.

“We didn't do anything,” Sam said.

“You're standing next to an overturned bin.”

“Correlation isn't causation.”

Andrey looked at him.

For the first time that morning, he seemed impressed.

“Okay.”

Sam grinned. “Was that good?”

“No.”

“Oh.”

Further down the corridor, Charlie arrived.

The noise seemed to change around him.

Not because people respected him. Quite the opposite.

Charlie was rich, and everyone knew it. He had the particular confidence of someone who had never needed to check the price before ordering something.

Beside him walked Ben Tuson.

Tuson laughed at something Charlie said.

Nobody else did.

Charlie pulled out his phone.

“Look at this.”

Tuson's attention immediately shifted.

“Is that the new one?”

“Yeah.”

“Mad.”

Charlie smiled. “Limited edition.”

Tuson's enthusiasm increased accordingly.

It was subtle. Almost invisible.

Almost.

From the end of the corridor, Violet Turner watched them.

Isla stood beside her.

The twins were universally considered sweet. Polite. Helpful. The kind of people teachers trusted instinctively.

They smiled when spoken to. They held doors open. They remembered people's birthdays.

And nobody noticed the way Violet's eyes followed the corridor.

Nobody noticed the calculation behind Isla's smile.

Nobody noticed that they had already worked out which students could be manipulated, which teachers could be persuaded, and which secrets would be most valuable.

Nobody except, perhaps, Simeon.

He stood at the lockers, arms folded.

Simeon was aggressive in the way thunderstorms were aggressive: nobody questioned whether something was going to happen.

The only question was when.

His eyes moved from the twins to Charlie.

Then to Tuson.

Then to Andrey.

Something wasn't right.

He just didn't know what.

Yet.

“Move.”

The voice came from behind him.

Simeon turned.

Samuel.

For half a second, the corridor became quieter.

Not silent.

Just quieter.

There was history between them.

A fight once.

Neither had won.

Neither had forgotten.

Samuel looked at him.

Simeon looked back.

Then Aaron appeared between them. Nobody knew how Aaron had arrived. One moment he wasn't there. The next he stood calmly between the two boys, hands folded behind his back.

Aaron was a Chinese monk.

At least, that was what everyone called him. Nobody knew how seriously he took it.

He looked at Simeon.

Then Samuel.

Then the overturned bin.

“Conflict,” Aaron said calmly, “is usually caused by attachment.”

Sam nodded.

“Yeah.”

Dersim nodded too.

“Definitely.”

Andrey glanced at them.

“You don't know what he means.”

“No,” said Sam.

“But it sounded wise.”

“It did.”

Aaron walked away.

Simeon watched him go.

Then he looked back at Samuel.

“Later.”

Samuel nodded.

“Later.”

The bell rang again.

Everyone began moving.

Except the Turner twins.

Isla waited until the corridor emptied.

Violet leaned towards her.

“Did you hear?”

“Hear what?”

“About the exam.”

Isla smiled.

“No.”

“You're lying.”

“So are you.”

They looked at each other.

Then both laughed.

Quietly.

At the other end of the corridor, Andrey stopped.

He had heard them.

Not enough to understand.

Enough to remember.

He turned.

The twins were already walking away.

For perhaps the first time all day, Andrey wasn't thinking about school being pointless.

He was thinking about a question.

Why would two people who had nothing to gain from lying...

lie?

Andrey didn't forget the twins.

He simply decided there were more important things to worry about.

By the next morning, the entire school seemed to have forgotten the conversation.

Andrey hadn't.

Neither had Simeon.

Something about the way Violet and Isla had spoken bothered him.

Not what they had said.

What they hadn't said.

At lunch, Andrey sat with Sam, Dersim, Spiros and Oliver.

Oliver was revising.

Sam was eating.

Dersim was attempting to balance a spoon on his nose.

Spiros was staring across the cafeteria.

Andrey noticed.

“What?”

Spiros looked away.

“Nothing.”

“You were staring.”

“Wasn't.”

“You were.”

Sam looked over.

“Who were you looking at?”

Spiros didn't answer.

Dersim lowered the spoon.

“I think he's in love.”

Spiros looked at him.

“I'm not.”

“That's exactly what someone in love would say.”

Oliver sighed.

“Can we please focus?”

Andrey looked towards the windows.

The weather had changed.

Dark clouds were moving over the school.

He didn't know why, but he had a feeling something was coming.

And he was right.

Because that afternoon, the school announced Sports Day.

The announcement spread through the school almost instantly.

Six schools.

One trophy.

Dozens of events.

Everyone wanted to win.

For most people, Sports Day was simply another school event.

For some, it was personal.

Simeon wanted to prove he was the strongest.

Oliver wanted to prove preparation mattered.

Charlie wanted his school to win because losing wasn't something he was particularly familiar with.

Sam and Dersim simply wanted something interesting to happen.

Andrey wasn't sure what he wanted.

Until he saw the Turner twins watching the announcement from the back of the room.

They weren't excited.

They weren't nervous.

They were calculating.

Andrey looked at them.

Violet smiled.

Isla smiled too.

Then they walked away.

Andrey followed them with his eyes.

Something was going to happen at Sports Day.

He just didn't know what.

The day of Sports Day arrived.

The field was packed.

Students filled the stands.

Teachers moved between events.

The three schools gathered around the athletics track.

The scoreboard stood in the middle of the field.

And the competition began.

Spiros hated mornings.

Not because he was tired. He was rarely tired.

He hated them because mornings at school were predictable.

The same corridors. The same bells. The same teachers pretending they weren't already exhausted. The same students walking around as if they had somewhere important to be.

Then there was the other problem.

Everyone seemed to like Spiros.

He couldn't walk through the sixth-form corridor without somebody saying his name. Girls whispered when he passed. Someone had once asked for his Instagram while he was literally standing in front of a teacher. Another had mysteriously developed an interest in Greek history after learning where he was from.

Spiros found the whole thing slightly ridiculous.

He was Greek. He was confident. He knew he was considered attractive. None of that particularly interested him.

Because there was one person he actually liked.

And nobody knew who.

Nobody except, perhaps, Harrison.

And maybe Andrey.

Spiros wasn't sure about Andrey.

That was the problem with Andrey. He didn't seem to notice things in the normal way. He simply observed them, stored them somewhere in his mind, and occasionally produced a conclusion that made everyone else wonder how he'd got there.

Spiros suspected Andrey had figured it out.

He hoped he hadn't.

The school gates opened at eight.

By eight-thirty, the entire school was already talking about Sports Day.

It wasn't an ordinary Sports Day.

Six schools were competing, and the trophy had become something of a legend. The previous year, their school had lost by three points.

Three.

Nobody had forgotten.

The PE department had spent weeks preparing.

The students had spent approximately twelve minutes preparing.

Spiros walked onto the athletics field and immediately realised there was a problem.

The school had been divided into factions.

The serious athletes had formed one group near the track. The academic students were gathered near the seating area. The sixth formers had taken over one corner of the field. Everyone else seemed to be arguing over who was supposed to be competing in what.

Oliver was standing with a clipboard.

Of course he was.

“Everyone needs to report to their events in the next five minutes,” he announced.

Nobody listened.

Andrey was sitting on the grass.

Of course he was.

“You're competing today,” Spiros said as he approached.

Andrey looked up.

“Apparently.”

“You look excited.”

“I'd rather be sleeping.”

Spiros laughed.

Oliver appeared behind them.

“You can't just sit there.”

“I'm not stopping anyone.”

“You're meant to be warming up.”

“I'll warm up when necessary.”

Oliver stared at him.

Spiros could practically see the argument forming.

Then a whistle blew.

Everyone moved.

For about thirty seconds.

Then everything went wrong.

Dersim had somehow wandered into the wrong event.

Sam was arguing with a teacher.

Simeon was loudly insisting that his race should have been earlier.

Charlie was attempting to convince someone that his school had better equipment.

Tuson was agreeing with him.

Ben Negus was explaining why everyone else was approaching the competition incorrectly.

The Turner twins stood together near the track.

Smiling.

Watching.

Spiros noticed something strange.

They weren't watching the races.

They were watching the other students.

Then Dersim ran towards Andrey.

“ANDREY.”

Andrey looked up.

“What?”

“I've discovered something.”

Andrey sighed.

“That's never a good sentence.”

“There's magic.”

Silence.

Spiros looked at Sam.

Sam looked at Dersim.

Oliver lowered his clipboard.

Andrey simply stared at him.

Dersim pointed towards the track.

“I saw it.”

“Saw what?”

“Magic.”

Oliver laughed.

“There's no such thing as magic.”

Dersim looked offended.

“You're just saying that because you're a neek.”

Oliver's expression tightened.

Andrey stood up.

“Show me.”

Dersim grabbed his arm.

“No, because you'll think I'm stupid.”

“I already think you're stupid.”

“Oh.”

Dersim paused.

“Fair.”

They walked towards the far side of the field.

Spiros followed.

He wasn't sure why.

Maybe curiosity.

Maybe because something about Dersim's expression didn't look like his usual nonsense.

They reached the equipment shed.

Dersim pointed behind it.

“Look.”

Nothing.

Just grass.

A fence.

A discarded athletics cone.

Andrey crouched down.

“What exactly am I looking for?”

“I don't know.”

“Excellent.”

Dersim pointed again.

“There.”

Spiros saw it.

For less than a second, the air seemed to shimmer.

Then it disappeared.

Andrey's expression didn't change.

Dersim stared at him.

“You saw it.”

“Yes.”

“You believe me?”

“No.”

Dersim frowned.

“But you saw it.”

“I said I saw something.”

“That's basically believing me.”

Oliver raised an eyebrow.

“See? This is why I told Andrey. He's naturally smart.”

Oliver raised an eyebrow.

“And I'm not?”

“You revise.”

Oliver looked genuinely offended.

Andrey ignored them.

He was staring at the place where the shimmer had appeared.

Spiros stepped closer.

“What was that?”

Andrey didn't answer.

Dersim whispered:

“Magic.”

Andrey finally looked at him.

“I know.”

Dersim's face went completely blank.

“You…”

“I know.”

“You knew about magic?”

“Yes.”

“How?”

Andrey looked across the field.

“Because I've seen it before.”

Spiros felt the atmosphere change.

The races continued in the distance.

Students shouted.

Teachers blew whistles.

Someone fell over a hurdle and immediately got back up pretending nothing had happened.

But none of them were watching anymore.

Andrey was looking towards the school buildings.

Specifically, towards the windows of the oldest block.

Spiros followed his gaze.

“What's in there?”

“I don't know yet.”

Dersim swallowed.

“So there really is magic?”

Andrey nodded.

“Yes.”

“Is it dangerous?”

“Probably.”

Dersim considered this.

“Should we tell someone?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because nobody would believe us.”

Spiros looked at him.

“What are we supposed to do?”

Andrey looked back towards the track.

“Win.”

Spiros frowned.

“What?”

“The Sports Day.”

“That has nothing to do with this.”

“Maybe.”

Andrey's eyes moved across the field.

The other schools were already dominating.

Their best runners were losing.

Their relay team was disorganised.

Their high jumpers were arguing.

Their long jumpers weren't even in position.

And their students were too busy fighting amongst themselves to notice.

The trophy was slipping away.

Andrey stood.

“We're losing.”

Spiros nodded.

“So?”

Andrey looked around.

“Everyone here thinks they're better than everyone else.”

Spiros looked around.

The athletes thought the academics were useless.

The academics thought the athletes were unintelligent.

The sixth formers thought the younger students were annoying.

The younger students thought the sixth formers were arrogant.

Charlie thought money solved most problems.

Ben Negus thought arguments could solve everything.

Dersim and Sam seemed to think nothing mattered.

The Turner twins smiled as if none of it concerned them.

And Simeon looked ready to fight somebody.

Andrey spoke quietly.

“If we keep competing against each other, the other schools win.”

Spiros looked at the scoreboard.

They were behind.

Badly.

Then the whistle blew.

Another event began.

And for the first time that day, everyone stopped talking.

They watched.

They realised the same thing.

They were losing.

And the strangest part was that, somewhere behind the athletics field, something impossible was moving through the air.

Magic was real.

The trophy was slipping away.

And the only way either problem could be solved was if everyone stopped fighting each other long enough to work together.

The question was whether they could do it.

Andrey looked across the field.

“Alright.”

He clapped once.

“Everyone. Together.”

And the entire school turned towards him.

And the entire school turned towards him.

For a moment, Andrey wasn't sure what to say next. Everyone was looking at him as if he had just volunteered them for something completely insane. Oliver still had his clipboard in his hands, Sam and Dersim were whispering to each other, and Spiros stood slightly behind them, watching the scoreboard.

Then Spiros smiled.

“So we win.”

Andrey nodded. “We win.”

The next event was announced over the speakers. The 200 metres. Spiros immediately looked towards the track.

“That one's mine.”

Andrey nodded. “Then win it.”

Spiros walked towards the starting line while the rest of their school gathered around the track. For the first time that day, they weren't scattered into separate groups. The athletes were encouraging each other. The students who weren't competing were shouting from the sidelines. Even Oliver had stopped worrying about organising everyone individually.

The gun went off.

Spiros exploded forward.

He took the lead almost immediately, but one of the runners from another school stayed close behind him. They were almost level coming into the final straight, and the crowd became louder with every step. Spiros pushed harder, crossing the line only a fraction of a second ahead.

Their side of the field erupted.

Sam jumped up. Dersim shouted something that nobody could understand. Oliver immediately checked the scoreboard.

“We gained four points.”

Andrey looked at the numbers.

“Good.”

“Very good,” Oliver corrected.

The next few events went much the same way. The three schools fought for every point. Their school dominated one event, another school answered with a victory of their own, and the third school kept stealing points in events nobody had expected them to win.

The scoreboard seemed to change every few minutes.

First.

Second.

First again.

Nobody knew who was going to win anymore.

Then, halfway through the afternoon, a tiny figure appeared near the edge of the field.

Andrey noticed him because he looked completely out of place amongst the other students.

He was incredibly small. Almost everyone around him was noticeably taller, making him look even smaller than he actually was. But his eyes immediately caught Andrey's attention. They were unusually large, dark and expressive, and even from a distance they made him look as though he was permanently on the verge of crying.

Then he started crying.

A student from one of the opposing schools noticed.

“Are you alright?”

“Yeah.”

“You're crying.”

“I know.”

“What's wrong?”

“I just don't think we're going to win.”

The student immediately softened.

“Don't worry. There's still loads of events left.”

Another student came over.

“Yeah. You'll be fine.”

The boy nodded.

“Thanks.”

His name was Dom.

And within minutes, people had begun gathering around him.

At first Andrey thought it was simply because everyone felt sorry for him. Then he noticed something strange.

A runner from their school was standing at the starting line, but instead of watching the starter, he was looking towards Dom.

A high jumper had stopped preparing for his attempt.

One of their relay runners was talking to Dom instead of getting into position.

Then the gun went off.

Their runner reacted late.

The other school won.

Oliver stared at the track.

“What just happened?”

Andrey looked at Dom.

“I think we have a problem.”

It wasn't simply that people were abandoning their events. That would have been easier to deal with. Dom seemed to make people lose focus. They still competed, but they weren't performing properly. They hesitated. They made mistakes. They became distracted by him, worried about him, or simply felt too guilty to ignore him.

The effect spread through the team.

A baton was dropped.

A runner started too slowly.

A jumper fouled their attempt.

A student who had been expected to score comfortably finished near the back.

The scoreboard changed.

Their school dropped from first to second.

Then third.

Then fourth.

Dersim stared at it in disbelief.

“Fourth?”

Sam looked at Oliver.

“How did we go from first to fourth?”

Oliver looked genuinely annoyed.

“Everyone's underperforming.”

Spiros looked towards Dom.

“That tiny guy?”

Andrey nodded.

“He's doing something.”

Dom looked over at them.

His huge eyes met Andrey's.

He immediately started crying harder.

Spiros looked away.

“No.”

Andrey glanced at him.

“What?”

“I refuse to feel bad for him.”

Dom sniffed.

Spiros covered his face.

“Stop.”

Andrey almost laughed.

Dom wasn't threatening anyone. He wasn't even telling people to stop competing. He simply existed, looked upset, and somehow people couldn't bring themselves to ignore him.

Andrey realised that was what made him dangerous.

Their school regrouped.

They stopped reacting to Dom and focused entirely on the events. It wasn't easy. Every time someone looked over, Dom seemed to notice. Every time someone went near him, another person followed.

But slowly, the team recovered.

They won the next sprint.

Then they gained points in the long jump.

Then another athlete won a field event.

Spiros took part in another race and managed another victory after a close finish with one of the runners from the second school. The two exchanged a quick nod afterwards, both clearly exhausted.

The scoreboard was becoming increasingly close.

Oliver looked at the numbers for several seconds before speaking.

“If we win the next two events, we could get back into second.”

Oliver hesitated.

“Possible.”

Dersim grinned.

“That's all I needed to hear.”

Their long-jumper landed further than anyone expected. A third-school athlete managed to steal points at the last possible moment.

Every mistake mattered.

And every time their school seemed ready to climb back to the top, one of the other schools pulled ahead again.

But Andrey's plan was working.

The students were finally acting like one team.

Even Charlie had stopped arguing with everyone.

Even Simeon was encouraging people instead of threatening to fight them.

Even Sam and Dersim had somehow become useful.

“Second.”

They were close.

Very close.

The relay.

The event worth enough points to completely change the standings.

Abbie.
